# Writing read-through questions

How to write the **Reflect** and **Act** questions for a read-through plan
("The Book of ___"). See [naming.md](./naming.md) for the vocabulary.

A read-through day carries **nothing but the two questions**. No reflection, no
study notes, no passage context. The reader reads the raw passage and does the
thinking themselves. That means these two questions are the only guidance on the
day, and nothing downstream can catch a mistake in them.

Themed plans are a different animal and are generated by the prompt in
`apps/server/src/routes/generate.ts`. This document is for read-throughs, which
are hand-built content in `seed-data.ts`.

## The shape: anchor, then turn

- **Reflect anchors.** It points at the one thing in the chapter worth stopping
  on, the thing a reader would walk straight past, and it cannot be answered by
  someone who skimmed.
- **Act turns.** It takes what Reflect anchored and lands it.

One points at the text, one points at them. Do not write two rounds of pressure.
Without a reflection to frame the day, two accusatory questions leave a reader
with no help seeing what they just read.

## The setup

Each question is up to **two sentences of setup, then the ask**.

- Two is a **ceiling, not a quota**. Plenty of chapters need one.
- **No word limit.** The test is whether it reads naturally out loud. A word cap
  produces chopped, choppy prose; we do not use one.
- Natural rhythm is a **fuller setup and a short ask**. The setup carries the
  weight; the question itself usually lands in eight to twelve words.
- The setup **reports** what the text says. It never interprets. This clause is
  the only framing in the entire day.

## The turn belongs in the question

The setup stays on the text. The moment the reader realizes this is about them
happens in the question, where they meet it, never pre-announced in the setup.

Do not assert the personal claim in the setup and then ask about it. That
defuses the turn and repeats itself. Pick one, and it goes in the question.

Second person directly in the ask is good, even when slightly informal.
"What do you make of **you** being the object of almost every action here?"
beats the formally correct alternative, because it sounds like a person talking.

## Never invent an image inside the ask

The question uses the passage's own words or plain speech. If you coin a
metaphor in the setup and then ask about it, the reader has to decode your image
before they can answer, and most will not bother.

Bad, because "escort" and "destination" are the writer's invention, not the
text's, and nothing specific can be named in reply:

> ...What are you still treating like the destination when it was only ever the
> escort?

Bad, because a reader answers "American" and learns nothing:

> ...What passport have you been proudest to carry?

Better. Same setups, but the answer is a real thing a person can name:

> ...What are you still doing to stay on God's good side?

> ...What do you lead with when you want someone to think well of you?

> **The test: can the reader answer with a person, a habit, a thing they have
> been asking for, or a moment from last week?** If the honest answer is a
> concept, rewrite the question. Reusing an image the passage itself supplies is
> fine, and good: "who cut into your lane," "who is still on the other side of
> the wall."

## Where a question has to land

Concrete is not the same as deep. A question can be perfectly answerable and
still be about nothing. Every question has to come to rest in one of three
places:

- **Who God is**, or what he is actually like.
- **Who Jesus is**, and what he did.
- **Who you actually are underneath**, and what is going on inside you.

Behavior is a way in, never the destination. "What do you lead with when you
want someone to think well of you" is answerable and still shallow, because the
answer is impression management. "Where does your sense of being somebody
actually come from" is the same passage taken all the way down to identity.

> If the honest answer is a fact about someone's schedule or manners, go deeper.
> If it touches their sense of who they are, or changes their picture of God,
> the question is doing its job.

## The setup and the ask must come off the same observation

If the setup opens one door and the question walks through a different one, the
day does not hold together. Find the bridge in the text so the setup ends where
the question begins.

**If a second good observation exists, it goes in Act. Never staple it onto
Reflect.**

## Craft palette

Moves to reach into, never a checklist. Most days use **one or two**. Never all
of them, and don't run the same pair two days running. A day that uses none of
these and simply says the passage well beats a day that runs the table.

- **The detail they'd walk past.** Hand them one true fact about this chapter
  they'd never have found on their own, and let the passage reopen on it. The
  move is the FACT; what follows is only the payoff the fact buys. Paul had
  never been to Rome, so Romans is a letter to strangers rather than a
  correction. First Corinthians 13 was written to a church fighting over
  spiritual gifts, so the wedding reading is a rebuke. On a read-through this is
  usually what the setup is doing, since the two questions are the only teaching
  the day gets. It must be true and checkable, never invented, and never
  something they could have seen for themselves.
- **The overcorrection.** Name the problem, then name the wrong cure they're
  already reaching for. Two ditches, one road.
- **The ache.** Open on a felt question rather than a statement, so they admit
  it's about them before any explanation arrives.
- **Both sides.** Frame it so the person who does the thing and the person who
  avoids it are both caught. Leave no one un-implicated.
- **One illustration, never more.** At most one everyday picture in a day, and
  invent it. Never borrow a story from a preacher, a book, or a sermon.
- **Land where the passage lands.** The close belongs to the text, not to a
  template. Some chapters end in comfort, some in encouragement, some in a plain
  reality check, some in a charge. Read where this one comes to rest and end
  there, even when that means the last beat is only tender or only hard.
  Blunt-then-warm is one good shape, never the required one. Whatever it is,
  leave them something short enough to carry into the afternoon.

## Telling or asking

Decide this before writing, and land the day where the passage points.

- **Asking** — the text makes a demand on a life. Most passages. The questions
  press, and the day lands in the reader's life.
- **Telling** — the text reveals rather than requires: genealogies, construction
  and sacrifice detail, doxologies, throne scenes, apocalyptic imagery. The day
  lands on God's character or on understanding, and the questions ask the reader
  to reason about what they were shown.

> **Hard to apply is not the same as not asking.** Romans 9 is difficult to
> apply and unmistakably makes a demand. A census list does not. Never use
> "telling" as an escape from a passage that is uncomfortable to land.

Recall and comprehension questions are banned in both modes. A telling day still
has to cost the reader some thought.

## Act is a question by default

Despite the name, **Act is usually a question.** Make it an action only when the
passage makes the action the obvious response, roughly **two days in seven**.

Good candidates for an action: a chapter that ends on "more than you ask or
imagine" (pray something bigger), or "submit to one another" (lose an argument
on purpose). Everywhere else, ask.

When it is a question, it still has to be answerable with something concrete and
nameable, not a musing. Force a specific person, thing, or choice.

## Voice: spoken, not submitted

Write it the way a person would say it out loud, never the way a person would
hand it in. No essay register, no academic register.

- Contractions are welcome.
- Colloquial constructions are fine where they read naturally aloud.
- **Never** use em dashes. Reach for a colon, a semicolon, a comma, parentheses,
  or a new sentence.
- Banned: transitional connectives (furthermore, moreover, thus, in
  conclusion), hedging (arguably, one might argue), an elevated synonym where a
  plain word exists, any sentence at home in a term paper.
- Read every line back as speech. If you would not say it across a table,
  rewrite it.

## One register

Readers are roughly **13 or 14 through 50 and beyond**. There are no youth and
adult variants of a read-through. Write one register: direct enough for adults,
clear enough for teenagers. A wide-open ask is harder for a young reader than a
question that points somewhere, so let the setup hand them the handle.

## Fidelity

The passage decides everything. Never make it say something it does not say, and
never pick a detail to serve a point you brought with you.

Any striking claim in a setup (a word study, a piece of history, a cultural
detail) is **stated to the reader as fact and ships as fixed content**. Verify
it before it lands. Where a passage is genuinely disputed among orthodox
readers, do not pick a side.

## Worked example

Ephesians 1:1–14, day one.

**Reflect**
> In Greek, verses 3 through 14 are one unbroken sentence, because Paul starts
> listing what God has done and cannot find a place to stop. Notice who is doing
> the verbs: he chose, he blessed, he lavished, he sealed. What do you make of
> you being the object of almost every action here?

**Act**
> Right at the end Paul says you were sealed, and the word he picks is a
> business term: the deposit that legally bound a buyer to finish the purchase.
> God put money down on you. Where are you still living like the deal might fall
> through?

Two sentences of setup each. The setup stays on Paul and on God; both turns land
in the question. Act builds on Reflect rather than starting a new topic: the
first says he did all of it, the second says he also guaranteed it.

## Checklist

1. Setup is two sentences or fewer, and reads naturally out loud.
2. The setup reports the text; it does not interpret it.
3. The setup and the ask come off the same observation.
4. The personal turn is in the question, not pre-announced in the setup.
5. The question does not repeat what the setup just said.
6. Act builds on Reflect instead of starting over.
7. Decided telling or asking, and the day lands there.
8. Neither question is answerable by someone who skimmed, or by recall alone.
9. Act is a question unless this is one of the days an action is obviously right.
10. No em dashes anywhere.
11. Every factual claim in a setup has been verified.
